# General Reflective Question Bank

A broad, non-chakra-framed reflective question bank covering: past experiences,
emotions, thoughts, healthy habits, beliefs, relationships, and career. Verified
against the existing `structured/quadrant_question_bank.json` -- this is
genuinely supplementary content (broader, more open-ended reflective prompts),
not a duplicate of the 10-quadrant structured intake questions.

Organized by theme, ~90 questions total. Full text preserved in this file
rather than reproduced here in the frontmatter description -- see the source
file for the complete set across 7 themes: Past, Emotions, Thoughts, Healthy
Habits, Beliefs, Relationships, and Career.

## Usage note
These are general-purpose reflective prompts, not chakra-specific or
diagnostic. Safe for supplementary retrieval alongside the structured
assessment flow, but should not be presented as a required or sequential
protocol -- they were not authored with the same
opening/deepening/scaling/exception structure used in
`chakra_specific_questioning_*.md`. A therapist should confirm phrasing
quality before production use -- several questions in the source were
somewhat awkwardly phrased (e.g. machine-translated or AI-drafted register),
worth a light copy-edit pass.

Practical, equipment-free music-based self-regulation activities the therapist
provided directly, organized by population/context. Unlike most of the raga
and chakra practitioner content elsewhere in this knowledge base, this is
population-segmented practical guidance (what to actually do), which fills a
real gap: most of the `music_therapy_interventions` structured data has
technique *names* but blank tempo/duration/session-structure fields, since no
prior source specified that level of practical detail. This document does.

## Prenatal Activities

1. **Humming meditation** — Sit or lie comfortably, close your eyes, and hum a low, steady note for a few breaths at a time. The vibration is soothing for the parent and gently reaches the baby. Try humming through an exhale, pausing, then repeating for 5–10 minutes.
2. **Singing to the baby** — Sing lullabies, favorite songs, or made-up tunes softly to the belly. Babies begin recognizing their mother's voice in the womb; this builds an early bond while calming the parent's nervous system.
3. **Breath-and-voice pairing** — Combine slow breathing with soft vocal sounds: inhale for 4 counts, exhale while sighing out an "ahh" or "ohh" sound. Blends vocal music therapy with relaxation breathing, useful for reducing anxiety.
4. **Vocal toning for relaxation** — Sit upright, take a deep breath, and let out an open vowel sound ("aa", "ee", "oo") slowly and steadily. Repeat with different vowels; used to release tension and calm the mind.
5. **Call-and-response singing with a partner** — Take turns singing simple phrases back and forth, even nonsense syllables. Playful, connects with a partner, doubles as light social-emotional engagement.

## For School Students (no materials/equipment needed)

1. **Humming break** — Between homework sessions, hum a favorite tune for 2–3 minutes with eyes closed. Resets focus and calms pre-exam jitters.
2. **Body percussion rhythms** — Clap, tap thighs, or stomp feet in simple repeating patterns ("clap-clap-tap-tap"). Burns off restless energy, improves concentration afterward.
3. **Singing along to memory** — Recall and sing a favorite song from memory. Boosts mood and works memory recall simultaneously.
4. **Breath-and-hum combo** — Inhale slowly for 4 counts, hum out the exhale as long as comfortable, repeat 5–6 times. Useful before tests or when anxious.
5. **Call-and-response with a sibling/friend** — Take turns singing/chanting short phrases back and forth. Builds listening skills, turns a break into social activity.
6. **"Soundtrack your mood" reflection** — Think of a song matching current feelings, hum or sing a bit of it. Helps name and process emotions in a low-pressure way.
7. **Rhythmic recitation** — Turn multiplication tables, spellings, or notes into a rhythmic chant. Combines music with memory retention for studying.
8. **Vocal toning for stress release** — Deep breath, long "aaa"/"ooo" sound, slowly. A few repetitions when overwhelmed; under a minute.
9. **Freeze dance (mental version)** — Imagine a favorite upbeat song, move/sway to it seated or standing, "freeze" when mentally pausing the song. Quick energizer between study blocks.
10. **Group humming circle** (if with family/friends) — Everyone hums together into one long note. Builds connection and calm, especially after a stressful day.

## For College Students

1. **Humming reset between study blocks** — Low, steady note for 2–3 minutes between lectures/sessions.
2. **Breath-and-hum for exam anxiety** — Inhale 4 counts, hum out the exhale, 5–6 rounds before an exam/presentation — activates the parasympathetic nervous system.
3. **Singing through emotional processing** — Sing/hum a song matching current mood (stress, homesickness, excitement) to acknowledge and release rather than suppress the emotion.
4. **Rhythmic chanting for memorization** — Turn dense material (definitions, formulas, dates) into a rhythmic chant or tune.
5. **Vocal toning for overwhelm** — Long, slow "aaa"/"ooo" exhalations for a minute; effective during finals/deadline weeks.
6. **Internal music imagery** — Mentally "play" a favorite song in full detail (melody, lyrics, instruments); useful winding down before sleep or between classes.
7. **Call-and-response with roommates/friends** — Trade short sung/chanted phrases; low-effort social connection, breaks up isolation during intense study periods.
8. **Body percussion for restlessness** — Clap or tap a rhythm on desk/thighs when too wired to sit still.
9. **"Playlist in your head" mood shift** — Recall an upbeat/motivating song, hum/sing it to shift energy before a workout or hard task.
10. **Group humming or singing** (if living with others) — Eases dorm/roommate tension, builds community during stressful periods.
11. **Pre-sleep vocal wind-down** — Hum or sing quietly before bed; helps with racing thoughts common during exam season, supports sleep.

## For Neurodivergent People

*General principles that apply across all conditions below:*
- **No "correct" form.** Volume, pitch, duration, and whether audible or internal should be self-determined.
- **Work with existing regulating behaviors, don't replace them.** Stimming, echolalia, tics, and repetitive movement are natural regulators — pair music with them, don't substitute for them.
- **Masking isn't the goal.** These are private, low-demand tools, not performance.
- **Sensory profile decides the activity, not the diagnosis label.** Two autistic people might need opposite things — these are starting points, not prescriptions.
- **Stop anytime something feels aversive rather than regulating.** What helps can shift day to day or hour to hour.

**For sensory regulation (autism, sensory processing differences):**
1. Self-paced humming — adjust pitch/volume freely, no fixed rules.
2. Silent internal music — mentally "play" a comforting song without vocalizing, for when sound is overstimulating.
3. Rhythmic stimming — rock, sway, or flap in time with a hummed/imagined rhythm.
4. Predictable repetition loops — hum the same short phrase repeatedly; sameness itself is calming.

**For focus and restlessness (ADHD):**
5. Rhythmic tapping for transitions — steady beat on leg/desk to bridge between tasks.
6. "Study chant" — turn facts or to-do items into a rhythmic chant to hold attention.
7. Freeze-and-release humming — hum energetically for 30 seconds, then sit in quiet; repeat as a focus reset.
8. Movement-paired humming — hum while pacing/fidgeting rather than sitting still, since stillness can work against focus for some.

**For motor coordination (dyspraxia/DCD):**
9. Simple rhythm tapping — one steady beat with one hand only, no inter-limb coordination required.
10. Vocal-only rhythm — chant/hum a rhythm instead of clapping/tapping if fine-motor timing feels effortful.
11. Slow-tempo humming — self-set pace rather than matching external timing.

**For tics (Tourette's/tic disorders):**
12. Tic-friendly humming — hum/vocalize alongside tics rather than suppressing them; may reduce tic frequency by giving the urge somewhere to go.
13. Rhythmic breathing without breath-holding — gentle hum-on-exhale, since breath-holding can worsen tic urges for some.

**For emotional regulation (OCD, anxiety-linked neurodivergence):**
14. Vocal toning for intrusive-thought interruption — sustained "aaa"/"ooo" can interrupt looping thoughts by shifting attention to physical sensation.
15. Grounding hum — hum while naming 3 things you can feel; pairs music with sensory grounding.

**For reading/processing differences (dyslexia, auditory processing differences):**
16. Turning text into rhythm — chant/sing information rather than reading silently.
17. Slowed-down internal singing — mentally sing information slowly, giving processing time without pressure.

**Additional neurodivergent-specific tools (co-regulation, special interests):**
- Stimming to internal rhythm — combine an existing stim with a hummed/imagined rhythm rather than suppressing either.
- Echoing/vocal stimming — lean into existing echolalia-style repetition intentionally as a calming tool.
- Special interest soundtrack (mental) — recall and hum/sing theme music or character songs tied to a special interest.
- Silence-friendly toning — humming with mouth closed and minimal volume, for internal-vibration benefit without much sound output.
- Co-regulation humming (optional, only with trusted people) — hum/chant alongside a trusted person without needing to talk or match pitch/rhythm exactly.

## For Senior Citizens

**Memory and cognitive engagement:**
1. Singing familiar songs from memory — hymns, folk songs, film songs, wedding songs. Long-term musical memory often stays strong even with recent-memory decline, valuable for mild cognitive decline or early dementia.
2. "Name that memory" reflection — hum/sing a tune, then discuss a memory it brings up. Combines music with reminiscence therapy.
3. Call-and-response with a caregiver/family member — old back-and-forth folk-singing style, encourages engagement without needing to remember full lyrics alone.

**Physical ease (seated/low-mobility friendly):**
4. Seated humming with gentle sway — hum while gently swaying side to side in a chair.
5. Hand-tapping to rhythm — tap fingers/palm lightly in time with a hummed tune; accessible with limited hand strength or mild arthritis (keep gentle).
6. Breath-and-hum for relaxation — slow inhale, gentle hum on exhale, repeated; useful for calming anxiety, aiding sleep, easing evening restlessness.

**Emotional wellbeing and social connection:**
7. Group singing (if with family/shared living) — strongly linked to reduced loneliness and improved mood in older adults.
8. "Song of the day" ritual — one favorite song hummed/sung during a daily routine, builds comforting structure.
9. Generational song sharing — seniors and younger family members trade old and current songs.

**Sleep and evening wind-down:**
10. Pre-sleep humming — soft humming or slow lullaby-style tune before bed, especially for restlessness/difficulty settling.
11. Silent internal music — mentally replaying a cherished old song during quiet moments (waiting rooms, resting in bed).

## For Any Adults

**Chronic pain / chronic illness (e.g., arthritis, fibromyalgia, long-term fatigue conditions):**
1. Slow humming for pain distraction — shifts attention from pain signals to sound/vibration; a coping tool **alongside, not instead of, medical management**.
2. Seated or lying-down breath-hum — for fatigue/low-energy days when movement-based coping isn't possible.
3. Rhythmic tapping within pain-free range — light tapping on a comfortable (not painful) body part.

**Anxiety and stress-related conditions:**
4. Vocal toning for panic/anxiety spikes — long, slow "aaa"/"ooo" exhale, 4–5 repetitions, to down-regulate the nervous system during acute anxiety.
5. Grounding hum — hum while naming physical sensations (feet on floor, hands, breath).
6. Predictable repetition — the same short phrase repeated rather than varied, for high-anxiety periods.

**Depression / low mood:**
7. "One song" rule — on low-motivation days, commit to just humming/singing one line of one song.
8. Memory-song reflection — a song tied to a positive memory, to gently counter flatness/numbness without forcing false positivity.
9. Movement-paired humming — hum while doing a small physical task (tea, tidying) to pair music with gentle behavioral activation.

**Sleep issues / insomnia:**
10. Pre-sleep humming ritual — a consistent wind-down cue signaling sleep is coming.

**Lifestyle-related conditions (e.g., stress tied to diabetes, hypertension, weight management):**
11. Stress-reset humming before meals/routines — as part of a broader routine, **not a treatment itself**.
12. Rhythmic breathing during cravings/urges — a minute of hum-on-exhale breathing to help ride out an urge, for those managing stress-eating/smoking-type patterns.

**General emotional processing:**
13. "Soundtrack the feeling" — hum/sing whatever matches the current emotional state, even if heavy or sad, as acknowledgment rather than needing to shift mood.
14. Co-regulation with a trusted person — humming/singing quietly together without needing to discuss what's wrong.

## For Specific Emotional Challenges

**Anger / frustration:**
1. Loud release humming — a forceful hum or strong "aaa" sound for a few seconds, a controlled release through voice rather than action.
2. Fast rhythmic tapping — quick, hard rhythm on thigh/desk to burn off frustration before it builds.
3. Growl-and-release — a short, low growl/grunt followed by a slow exhale hum.
4. Stomp-and-hum — a few stomps paired with a hum, if space allows.

**Sadness:**
5. Sit-with-it humming — a slow, low tune matching the sadness rather than forcing cheer.
6. Memory-song comfort — a song tied to comfort or nostalgia.
7. Quiet tears-and-tune — a soft hum continuing underneath crying, even shaky or broken.

**Loneliness:**
8. Singing to fill the space — out loud, even alone.
9. "Imagined duet" — humming a song while imagining singing it with someone you care about.
10. Call out into silence — sing a phrase and pause, as if waiting for a response.

**Irritation / low-grade annoyance:**
11. Short vocal sigh-hum — one long exhale with a hum/sigh sound.
12. Pattern-breaking hum — a completely different tune than what's looping in your head, to interrupt the cycle.

**Low mood (flat/heavy, not sadness exactly):**
13. One-line commitment — hum/sing just one line of any song, no pressure to continue.
14. Upbeat memory hum — a song from a genuinely fun or light-hearted time, even half-heartedly.

**Boredom:**
15. Freestyle humming — an unstructured, goal-free made-up tune.
16. Rhythm games with yourself — tap a pattern, repeat it, then vary it slightly.
17. Soundtrack your surroundings — hum a tune matching whatever you're looking at or doing.
